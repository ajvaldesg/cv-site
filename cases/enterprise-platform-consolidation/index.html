Case 03 · Platform

250 applications on 25 servers: consolidating without losing the business

Twenty-five servers carrying more than 250 web applications is not redundancy — it is duplication that nobody dares remove, because nobody is certain which application still matters. The work was to redesign the topology so that consolidation became safe, and governance became possible.

Context and constraints

The estate had grown by accretion: each initiative added an application, each application added a server, and each server added a licensing line. The result was a multi-server collaboration architecture where reliability was delivered by duplication rather than by design, and where governance meant asking people to stop doing what had always worked.

Three constraints shaped everything. The applications had to keep running — this was the platform people used to do their jobs. The workflow and reporting dependencies (K2, .NET applications, SQL Server Reporting Services and BDC/BCS integration into line-of-business systems) could not be treated as replaceable commodities. And any consolidation had to reduce the licensing footprint, or it would not survive its own business case.

The redesign keeps the application capabilities the business depends on — .NET applications, K2 workflow, SSRS reporting and BDC/BCS integration — while removing the duplication that made the estate expensive and unreliable. Governance is drawn as a first-class layer because, at this size, it is the only thing that keeps the topology from drifting back to where it started.

Decisions and trade-offs

Consolidate the topology before consolidating the applications

Removing 25 servers' worth of duplication is a topology problem first: once applications share a designed platform, decommissioning becomes an editorial decision rather than a leap of faith.

Accepted cost: a period where the new platform must host old and new workloads at the same time — the least glamorous phase of any consolidation, and the one that decides whether it works.

ReliabilityRisk sequencing

Keep the platform capabilities, remove the platform sprawl

K2, .NET, SSRS and BDC/BCS stay because the business processes depend on them. What is removed is the duplication around them, not the capability itself.

Accepted cost: the architecture must respect each technology's operating model instead of forcing a single uniform pattern onto all of them.

Business continuityCompatibility

Governance as a design requirement, not a post-migration clean-up

Ownership, change control and consolidation standards are part of the target state. Without them, a consolidated estate re-fragments the moment the next application arrives.

Accepted cost: slower onboarding for new applications, and a governance model that has to be maintained by people, not by tooling alone.

GovernanceSustainability

Licensing reduction as an architectural outcome

Fewer redundant servers and a consolidated topology reduce the licensing footprint — a business result that follows from the architecture rather than from a procurement negotiation.

Accepted cost: the consolidation must be complete enough to actually retire capacity; partial consolidation keeps the cost and adds the transition overhead.

Governance, security and scale

Reliability was the point of the redesign. Twenty-five servers is not resilience; it is a collection of single points of failure with different patch levels. A designed topology makes failure behaviour something the architecture specifies, rather than something the operations team discovers.

Governance protected the investment. Consolidation standards and application ownership are what stop the next ten applications from recreating the problem, and they are also what makes change control meaningful: you cannot govern an estate you cannot enumerate.

Scale defined the sequencing. With 250+ applications, consolidation is not a cut-over; it is a programme of decisions about what runs where, in what order, with which dependencies verified. The architecture's job was to make each of those decisions safe to take.

What this case demonstrates

Technical leadership on a consolidation that could not afford a big-bang failure: keeping the capabilities the business depends on, removing the duplication around them, and building governance into the target state so the result holds after the project ends. The measurable business outcome is structural — a reduced licensing footprint that follows from retiring redundant capacity.

Scope note: 250+ applications, 25 redundant servers and the reduced licensing footprint are the recorded facts for this engagement. No percentage savings, cost figures, team sizes or timelines are asserted.
